6. Circular Motion and Gravitation

Moving in a circle always requires a net force pointed at the center — everything in this section is a variation on that one fact.

Uniform Circular Motion

Make a prediction first: you whirl a ball on a string in a horizontal circle at constant speed. Its speed never changes — so is there any net force acting on it at all?

ac = v²/r   ·   Fc = mv²/r  (always pointed toward the center)

v — speed (constant in magnitude, but its direction is always changing)
r — radius of the circular path
ac — centripetal acceleration; Fc — centripetal force
ω — angular speed, ω = v/r

Yes — there is a net force, and it's the entire point of this section. Velocity is a vector; even at perfectly constant speed, a circling object's velocity direction is constantly changing, which is itself an acceleration. That acceleration points directly at the circle's center (never forward or backward along the path), so the net force causing it — tension, friction, gravity, whatever's actually supplying it — always points at the center too.

For math enthusiasts: why the acceleration points inward, not forward

Velocity is tangent to the circle at every instant. A short time later, the velocity vector has the same length but a slightly different direction — subtracting the two velocity vectors to get Δv gives a vector that, in the limit of a very short time interval, points exactly toward the center, perpendicular to the velocity itself. That's the geometric origin of "centripetal" ("center-seeking") acceleration, and working through the geometry carefully gives the exact formula ac = v²/r.

The instant tension disappears, the net force on the ball drops to zero — by Newton's First Law it then travels in a straight line at constant velocity, tangent to the circle at the exact point of release. It does not fly straight outward (radially), which is the most common misconception this demonstration corrects.

Newton's laws in their simple F = ma form only work directly in inertial (non-accelerating) frames; a rotating frame is accelerating (it's constantly changing direction), so to keep using F = ma-style bookkeeping inside it, you have to add fictitious terms — the centrifugal force here — that don't correspond to any actual push or pull. Banked Curves

Make a prediction first: a race track curve is banked (tilted inward) at just the right angle for a specific speed. At exactly that speed, how much friction does a car need from its tires to make the turn?

Ideal banking angle (no friction needed): tanθ = v²/(rg)

θ — the bank angle, measured from horizontal
v — the car's speed; r — the curve's radius
N — the normal force from the road, tilted inward with the bank

On a banked curve, the road's normal force itself tilts inward. Pick the bank angle so that the horizontal component of that tilted normal force alone supplies exactly the needed centripetal force — and the answer to the prediction is zero friction required at that one specific "design speed."

For math enthusiasts: deriving the safe speed range

Decomposing both the normal force and (up-slope or down-slope) friction into horizontal and vertical components and solving Newton's Second Law in each direction gives v² = rg(tanθ ± μ) / (1 ∓ μtanθ) — the "+"/"−" (top sign) case is the fastest safe speed, where friction acts down-slope to keep the car from sliding up and off; the "−"/"+" case is the slowest, where friction acts up-slope to keep the car from sliding down. Setting μ = 0 in either formula collapses both back to the single ideal-angle result from Widget 1.

Vertical Circles

Make a prediction first: a roller-coaster car goes around a vertical loop at constant track speed near the top. Is the normal force pushing on the rider from the track the same all the way around the loop, or does it change?

N = mv²/r + mg cosφ, where φ is the angle measured from the bottom of the loop

N — normal force from the track (the rider's apparent weight)
φ — position around the loop: 0° at the bottom, 180° at the top
v — speed at that point in the loop (changes with height, via energy conservation)

N changes continuously around the loop — largest at the bottom (where gravity and the centripetal requirement both point the same way, "up"), smallest at the top (where gravity now helps supply the centripetal force instead of fighting it). This tab develops the height-and-loop-radius relationship that Energy → Roller Coaster Physics already used, by tracking apparent weight around the whole circuit instead of just the top point.

For math enthusiasts: the full derivation, bottom to top

At angle φ from the bottom, height above the bottom is h(φ) = r(1−cosφ), so by energy conservation v(φ)² = vbottom² − 2gr(1−cosφ). Newton's Second Law along the radial direction then gives N = mv(φ)²/r + mgcosφ. Requiring N ≥ 0 everywhere around the loop turns out to be most restrictive exactly at the top (φ = 180°), which is why checking only the top point (as the Roller Coaster Physics tab did) is enough to guarantee the whole loop is safe.

Gravitation

Make a prediction first: the International Space Station orbits only about 400 km above Earth's surface — roughly a 10% increase in distance from Earth's center. Is gravity up there dramatically weaker than at the surface, or does something else entirely explain why astronauts float?

F = Gm₁m₂/r²

F — gravitational force between two masses
G — the gravitational constant, 6.674×10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg²
m₁, m₂ — the two masses; r — distance between their centers

For math enthusiasts: why the sliders use exponents of 10

Real astronomical masses span such an enormous range (a kilogram-scale probe next to a 5.97×10²⁴ kg planet) that a normal linear slider would be useless — nearly the entire slider would represent "basically zero" on a planetary scale. Sliding the exponent instead (10 raised to whatever the slider says) is a standard trick for exploring quantities across many orders of magnitude at once.

Orbits

Make a prediction first: a satellite is launched sideways, exactly fast enough to circle its planet. Launch a second, identical satellite from the same height but a little slower — does it just fall to a lower circular orbit, or does something else happen?

Circular speed: vcirc = √(GM/r)   ·   Escape speed: vesc = √(2GM/r) = vcirc√2

G — gravitational constant; M — mass of the central body
r — orbital distance from the center; v — orbital speed
An orbit is really just a very long fall that keeps missing the ground

For math enthusiasts: the four orbit shapes are all conic sections

Every possible gravitational orbit is a conic section, sorted purely by total mechanical energy: bound orbits (energy < 0) are ellipses, with the circle as the special case of zero eccentricity; the borderline (energy = 0, exactly escape speed) is a parabola; unbound orbits (energy > 0, faster than escape speed) are hyperbolas that never return. This is the same "conservation of energy decides the outcome" idea from Section 3, just applied to gravity specifically instead of springs or ramps.

For math enthusiasts: deriving Kepler's Third Law from circular orbits

For a circular orbit, the centripetal requirement mv²/r = GMm/r² combined with v = 2πr/T gives, after simplifying, T² = (4π²/GM)r³ — a constant (4π²/GM) times r³, exactly Kepler's Third Law. Using years and astronomical units around the Sun specifically, that constant works out to almost exactly 1, which is why T²/r³ ≈ 1 above regardless of r.
