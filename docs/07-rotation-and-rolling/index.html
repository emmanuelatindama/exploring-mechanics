7. Rotational Mechanics and Rolling

Every straight-line idea from Kinematics and Newton's Laws has a rotational twin — this section is that translation, one concept at a time.

Angular Kinematics

Make a prediction first: a spinning fan blade has every part turning at the exact same angular speed. Does a point near the tip move through space faster, slower, or at the same speed as a point near the hub?

v = rω   ·   ω = Δθ/Δt   ·   α = Δω/Δt

θ — angular position (radians); ω — angular velocity; α — angular acceleration
r — distance from the rotation axis; v — tangential (straight-line) speed at that radius

Every point on a spinning rigid body shares the exact same ω — that's what "rigid" and "spinning together" mean. But tangential speed v = rω depends on r too, so a point farther from the axis sweeps through more actual distance per second, moving faster in the ordinary sense, even while turning at the identical angular rate.

For math enthusiasts: the full linear-to-angular translation table

Every linear kinematics equation from Section 1 has a rotational twin, symbol for symbol: x → θ, v → ω, a → α. So v = u + at becomes ω = ω₀ + αt, and s = ut + ½at² becomes θ = θ₀ + ω₀t + ½αt². Nothing new needs deriving — the mathematics is identical, only the physical meaning of each symbol has changed.

Torque

Make a prediction first: you push a heavy door with the exact same force, first right next to the hinge, then out at the handle, then finally at the handle but pushing at a shallow angle instead of straight-on. Do all three pushes turn the door equally well?

τ = Frsinθ

τ — torque, the turning effect of a force about a pivot
F — the applied force; r — distance from the pivot to where it's applied
θ — the angle between the force and the lever arm (the line from pivot to push point)

Force alone doesn't determine how well something turns — where and at what angle you push matters just as much. Pushing right at the hinge (small r) barely turns the door no matter how hard you push; pushing at a shallow angle (small sinθ) wastes most of the force on a component that just presses along the door instead of turning it.

For math enthusiasts: torque as a cross product

τ = Frsinθ is the magnitude of the vector cross product τ⃗ = r⃗ × F⃗ — the sinθ factor is exactly what makes a force pointed directly along the lever arm (θ = 0° or 180°) produce zero torque no matter how large F is, since that force has no component actually perpendicular to the arm to do any turning.

Widget 2 — Balanced Torques Are Not the Same as Balanced Forces

Two equal, opposite forces at different points on a beam can balance in torque (no net spin) while the beam still accelerates sideways — or balance in force while still spinning faster and faster. They're two separate conditions, and satisfying one says nothing about the other.

Force 1, at the left end (up/down)20 N
Force 2, at the right end (up/down)-20 N
Net force (should be 0 for translational equilibrium)0 N
Net torque about the center (should be 0 for rotational equilibrium)—

For math enthusiasts: this pair is a pure "couple"

Two equal, opposite, parallel forces acting at different points — set both sliders to opposite equal values — form what's called a "couple": net force is always exactly zero (nothing accelerates the beam's center of mass anywhere), yet the net torque is not zero, so the beam spins in place, faster and faster, around its own center forever. It's the cleanest possible illustration that force balance and torque balance are independent bookkeeping.

Widget 3 — Equal Torque, Different Spin-Up

Apply the exact same torque to a hoop, a disk, and a sphere of the same mass and radius, all at once, and watch the sphere spin up fastest — same τ, but very different I = τ/α.

Moment of Inertia

Make a prediction first: a hoop and a solid disk, made of the same material with the same total mass and the same radius, race down the same ramp. Do they tie?

I = Σmiri²  (sums up every bit of mass, weighted by how far it sits from the axis)

I — moment of inertia: rotational inertia, the rotational analogue of mass
mi, ri — the mass of a tiny piece of the object and its distance from the rotation axis

Unlike ordinary mass, I depends on how that mass is arranged, not just how much there is. A hoop puts every bit of its mass out at the rim (all of it far from the axis); a solid disk spreads mass all the way from the center out to the rim. Same total mass, same radius, very different I — which is why they won't tie down that ramp (the full race is in the next tabs).

For math enthusiasts: why the hoop always wins (has the largest I)

Every one of these formulas has the form I = kmr² for some shape-dependent fraction k ≤ 1. The hoop has k = 1 — the theoretical maximum — because literally all of its mass sits at the full radius r. Every other shape has some mass closer to the axis than r, which can only pull the average r² (and so k) down, never up.

Rotational Energy

Make a prediction first: a solid sphere and a hoop, same mass and radius, are released from rest at the top of the same ramp and roll (without slipping) to the bottom. Do they arrive at the same speed?

KErot = ½Iω²   ·   Rolling object's total KE = ½mv² + ½Iω²

KErot — rotational kinetic energy, stored in spinning motion alone
I — moment of inertia; ω — angular speed
A rolling object has both translational KE (½mv²) and rotational KE (½Iω²) at once

Widget 1 — Flywheel Energy Storage

A flywheel is a purely rotational battery: no chemistry, just spin. Some buses and trains genuinely use flywheels to store braking energy and give it back on acceleration.

For math enthusiasts: why ½Iω² is the rotational twin of ½mv²

Split a spinning object into tiny mass pieces mi, each moving at its own tangential speed vi = riω. Total kinetic energy is Σ½mivi² = Σ½miri²ω² = ½ω²Σmiri² = ½Iω², using the moment-of-inertia definition directly. It's not a new law — it's ordinary KE = ½mv², added up piece by piece around the object.

For math enthusiasts: deriving the sphere's √(10gh/7)

Energy conservation: mgh = ½mv² + ½Iω². For rolling without slipping, ω = v/r, and for a solid sphere I = (2/5)mr². Substituting: mgh = ½mv² + ½(2/5)mr²(v/r)² = ½mv²(1 + 2/5) = (7/10)mv². Solving for v gives √(10gh/7) — mass and radius both cancel completely, so the result only depends on shape (through that 2/5), never on size or weight.

Rolling Without Slipping

Make a prediction first: a bowling ball is thrown down the lane sliding, with almost no spin at all. Does it slide all the way to the pins, or does something change partway down?

Rolling condition (no slipping): v = rω

v — the center's translational speed; ω — angular speed; r — radius
Static friction — enforces the rolling condition; it does zero work and costs zero energy while rolling continues without slipping

Widget 2 — Yo-Yo / Spool Model

A falling yo-yo is "rolling without slipping" along its own string — the string's contact point on the axle can't slip, which slows the fall dramatically compared to a plain dropped weight.

Outer radius, R0.03 m
Axle (string) radius, r0.005 m
Fall acceleration, g/(1+I/mr²)0.11 m/s²
Compare to free fall86× slower
For math enthusiasts: why the thin axle matters so much

Treating the yo-yo as a uniform disk, I = ½mR², so the fall acceleration is g/(1 + R²/2r²). Because the axle radius r is so much smaller than the body radius R, that ratio R²/r² is huge, making the whole denominator huge and the fall dramatically slower than free fall — exactly why yo-yos hang and spin for a satisfying few seconds instead of just dropping.

Widget 3 — Bowling Ball: From Sliding to Rolling

Answering the prediction: friction between the ball and the lane keeps decelerating its slide and speeding up its spin until v = rω is finally satisfied — at which point the ball switches to true rolling and friction's job is done.

Initial slide speed, v₀8 m/s
Initial spin (0 = pure slide)0 rad/s
Final true-rolling speed, (5/7)v₀ + (2/7)rω₀5.71 m/s
Speed lost to friction while skidding2.29 m/s
For math enthusiasts: why angular momentum about the contact point is conserved

Friction acts exactly at the contact point, so it exerts zero torque about that specific point (zero lever arm) — meaning angular momentum measured about the contact point stays constant throughout the skid, even though the ball's linear momentum and spin are each changing. Setting initial and final angular momentum about that point equal, then imposing the final rolling condition vf = rωf, gives the (5/7)v₀ + (2/7)rω₀ formula directly for a solid sphere.

Widget 4 — The Spool Paradox

Pull a spool's string at a shallow angle and it rolls toward you; pull it steeper and it reverses and rolls away — the exact same critical-angle idea (θc = arccos(r/R)) that made the double cone and other counterintuitive rolling problems tick.

Angular Momentum and Gyroscopes

Make a prediction first: a spinning bicycle wheel is held by its axle by someone sitting on a stool that spins freely. They flip the wheel upside down. Does anything happen to them, even though they never touched the stool?

L = Iω  (conserved whenever no external torque acts)

L — angular momentum
I — moment of inertia; ω — angular velocity (both are direction-sensitive here)
Precession — the slow sideways sweep of a spin axis under torque, instead of simply toppling

Widget 1 — Figure Skater: Finishing the Conservation Law

Section 7's Moment of Inertia tab showed I dropping when arms pull in. Here's the payoff: L = Iω has to stay fixed with no external torque, so if I drops, ω must climb to compensate — exactly, not approximately.

Body's own I (fixed)2.0 kg·m²
Arm mass (each)3 kg

For math enthusiasts: this is the rotational twin of momentum conservation

Just like linear momentum p = mv stays fixed with no external force, angular momentum L = Iω stays fixed with no external torque. Pulling arms in doesn't apply any external torque about the spin axis (the pulling force acts along a line through that axis), so L before and after must match exactly: Ioutωout = Iinωin.

Widget 3 — Gyroscopic Precession

A fast-spinning top under gravity doesn't fall over — its spin axis instead sweeps slowly around in a circle. Torque changes the direction of angular momentum here, not just its size, and the two vectors being perpendicular is exactly what turns "falling" into "precessing."

Spin angular momentum, L5.0 kg·m²/s
Tipping torque (from gravity), τ2.0 N·m
Precession rate, Ω = τ/L0.40 rad/s
What happensSlow, steady precession
For math enthusiasts: why faster spin means slower, steadier precession

Precession rate Ω = τ/L — for a fixed tipping torque, a larger spin angular momentum L gives a smaller Ω. That's why a fast top precesses slowly and looks almost stationary, while a nearly-stopped top wobbles wildly right before it topples — as L shrinks toward zero, precession rate blows up, and the simple "torque only changes direction" picture breaks down entirely.

Widget 4 — Tennis-Racket Theorem (Intermediate-Axis / Dzhanibekov Effect)

Spin a rigid body about its smallest or largest principal axis and it wobbles a little, stably, forever. Spin it about the intermediate axis instead, and even a tiny wobble grows until the whole body flips — the exact same "no external torque" angular-momentum bookkeeping as the rest of this tab, just with three axes instead of one.
