8. Oscillations, Resonance, and Chaos

Nearly anything given a small nudge away from a stable resting point oscillates back and forth in exactly the same mathematical way.

Simple Harmonic Motion

Make a prediction first: a mass on a spring is pulled twice as far from its resting position before being released. Does it complete each full back-and-forth cycle twice as slowly, since it now has twice as far to travel?

x(t) = Acos(ωt + φ)   ·   ω = √(k/m)   ·   T = 2π/ω

A — amplitude: the maximum displacement from equilibrium
ω — angular frequency; T — period (time for one full cycle); φ — phase (where in the cycle it starts)
k — spring constant; m — mass
Restoring force: always points back toward equilibrium, and (for SHM specifically) grows in direct proportion to displacement: F = −kx

The answer to the prediction is no — and that's the single most surprising fact about simple harmonic motion. Amplitude A doesn't appear anywhere in the period formula T = 2π√(m/k). A bigger swing does have farther to travel, but it also picks up proportionally more restoring force and speed along the way, and those two effects exactly cancel.

For math enthusiasts: why F = −kx leads to a cosine solution

Newton's Second Law with a linear restoring force gives m(d²x/dt²) = −kx, or d²x/dt² = −(k/m)x. The function that keeps reproducing itself (times a negative constant) under differentiation twice is exactly sine or cosine, which is why x(t) = Acos(ωt + φ) solves the equation for ω = √(k/m) — you can check this by differentiating it twice and substituting back in.

Pendulums

Make a prediction first: a heavy bowling ball and a light tennis ball hang from identical strings of the same length. Swung gently, does one swing back and forth faster than the other?

T = 2π√(L/g)  (for small swing angles)

T — period; L — pendulum length; g — local gravitational acceleration
Small-angle approximation — valid for swings up to roughly 15–20°; the period formula drifts noticeably beyond that

Mass doesn't appear anywhere in the small-angle period formula — the answer to the prediction is no, they swing at the same rate, regardless of weight. That's the same mass-cancellation trick from free fall, and for the same underlying reason: a heavier bob feels proportionally more restoring force and proportionally more inertia resisting it, and the two cancel exactly.

For math enthusiasts: the correction series, and physical vs. simple pendulums

The exact large-angle period is T = T₀(1 + θ₀²/16 + 11θ₀⁴/3072 + ⋯), with θ₀ in radians — a correction that grows with the square of the angle, which is why it's negligible for a small nudge but very much not for a wide swing. Separately, a real swinging object with size and shape (a "physical pendulum" — a swinging rod, a person on a swing) follows the same form with L replaced by I/(md), where I is its moment of inertia and d is the distance from the pivot to its center of mass — the "simple pendulum" (a point mass on a massless string) is the special case where all the mass already sits at that one distance.

Damping

Make a prediction first: a car's shock absorbers wear out and provide less resistance than they should. Does the car now bounce back to level faster after a bump, or slower?

ζ = c / (2√(mk))  — the damping ratio sorts every case into one of three regimes

ζ — damping ratio: ζ < 1 underdamped, ζ = 1 critically damped, ζ > 1 overdamped
c — damping coefficient (resistance strength); m — mass; k — spring constant

Counterintuitively, less damping doesn't mean a faster return to rest — it means the car overshoots level and oscillates before settling (underdamped). The fastest possible return without any overshoot at all is exactly ζ = 1 (critically damped) — piling on more damping than that (overdamped) actually slows the return back down again, just without any bounce.

For math enthusiasts: the three solution forms

Underdamped (ζ < 1): x(t) = Ae−ζωntcos(ωdt), with ωd = ωn√(1−ζ²) — an oscillation inside a decaying envelope. Critically damped (ζ = 1): x(t) = (A + Bt)e−ωnt — the fastest non-oscillating return. Overdamped (ζ > 1): a sum of two decaying exponentials, both slower than the critical case's — piling on damping trades oscillation for sluggishness.

Driven Oscillations

Make a prediction first: you push a playground swing at exactly its own natural back-and-forth rate versus at some random, mismatched rate. Does the timing of your pushes actually matter, as long as you're putting in the same effort?

Steady-state amplitude: A(ω) = F₀/m / √[(ωn²−ω²)² + (2ζωnω)²]

ω — the driving (pushing) frequency; ωn — the system's own natural frequency
F₀ — the strength of each push; δ — the phase lag between the push and the resulting motion

Timing matters enormously — that's the whole answer to the prediction. Push at nearly the natural frequency and even gentle pushes build up a large steady swing; push at a badly mismatched rate and you mostly fight the swing's own motion, getting a disappointing amplitude no matter how hard you push.

For math enthusiasts: the phase lag formula and its three landmarks

δ = atan[2ζωnω / (ωn² − ω²)]. Far below resonance (ω ≪ ωn), the motion follows the push almost instantly (δ ≈ 0). Far above resonance (ω ≫ ωn), the motion lags a full half-cycle behind (δ ≈ 180°) — you're effectively pushing against the swing more often than with it, which is exactly why the amplitude is small there too. Exactly at resonance, the lag is precisely 90°, the quarter-cycle timing that turns out to pump in energy most efficiently.

Try it for real: swing timing

  1. Push a playground swing at its own natural rate (matching its already-established rhythm) versus deliberately pushing too fast or too slow — the mismatched timing builds up almost no height even with equally hard pushes.

Resonance

Make a prediction first: soldiers marching in perfect step across a lightweight footbridge have, historically, occasionally caused the bridge to sway alarmingly or even fail — while the same soldiers walking normally (out of step) across it would not. What's different about marching in step?

Resonance: driving frequency ≈ natural frequency ⟹ amplitude peaks

Resonance — driving a system at (or very near) its own natural frequency, producing a dramatically larger steady-state response than any other driving frequency
Lower damping ⟹ a taller, narrower resonance peak; higher damping ⟹ a shorter, broader one

Marching in step means every footfall is a push delivered at the exact same frequency, over and over — the last tab's amplitude formula peaking right where driving frequency matches the bridge's own natural sway frequency. Normal out-of-step walking spreads footfalls across essentially random timing relative to the bridge's motion, which barely pumps in any energy at all.

For math enthusiasts: why the peak sits slightly below ωₙ

Maximizing A(ω) with respect to ω gives the peak exactly at ωpeak = ωn√(1 − 2ζ²), slightly below the natural frequency itself, with peak height growing roughly as 1/(2ζ) for small ζ. As ζ → 0, the peak frequency creeps back toward exactly ωn and the peak height blows up without bound — an undamped system driven exactly at resonance has no steady-state amplitude limit at all.

Coupled Oscillators and Nonlinear Motion

Make a prediction first: two double pendulums are released from angles that differ by less than a tenth of a degree — close enough to look identical to the eye. A minute later, are they still swinging in near-identical paths, or could they be doing something completely different from each other?

Two tuning forks at slightly different pitches, played together, produce a slow "wah-wah-wah" throb called beats — a simple, exactly-solvable stand-in for the same energy-sloshing idea behind two coupled pendulums trading swing amplitude back and forth. A double pendulum, meanwhile, is simple to describe and almost impossible to predict for long: it's chaotic.

Beat frequency — |f₁ − f₂|, the slow throbbing rate from combining two nearby frequencies
Normal modes — the special combined motions of a coupled system that do repeat cleanly, unlike a generic combination
Chaos — a deterministic system (no randomness anywhere in the equations) whose outcomes are still practically unpredictable because tiny input differences blow up over time

For math enthusiasts: the trig identity behind beats

sin(2πf₁t) + sin(2πf₂t) = 2cos(2π (f₁−f₂)⁄₂t) sin(2π (f₁+f₂)⁄₂t). Read the right side as a fast oscillation at the average frequency, whose amplitude is itself slowly modulated by a cosine at half the frequency difference — since amplitude modulation doesn't care about sign, the perceived beat rate is the full difference |f₁ − f₂|.

For math enthusiasts: why "chaotic" doesn't mean "random"

The double pendulum's equations of motion are completely deterministic — given exact initial conditions, the future is, in principle, exactly fixed. What makes it chaotic is that nearby starting points separate exponentially over time (roughly Δθ(t) ≈ Δθ₀eλt for a positive "Lyapunov exponent" λ), so any real-world uncertainty in your starting angle — no matter how tiny — eventually grows to swamp any prediction. It's the polar opposite of the orbit simulator from Section 6, where nearby starting speeds gave smoothly, predictably different (not wildly diverging) ellipses.
