5. Constraints and Simple Machines

Mechanical advantage and the principle that ideal machines trade force for distance.

Mechanical Advantage

Make a prediction first: a machine lets you lift a 200 kg load using only 40 kg worth of push. Does that mean the machine created extra energy from nowhere?

Input work = output work, for an ideal (frictionless) machine.

MA — mechanical advantage: output force ÷ input (effort) force
Fin, Fout — the effort force you apply and the load force delivered
din, dout — the distance your effort moves and the distance the load moves

No machine creates energy. A machine with mechanical advantage MA lets you push with 1/MA of the force the load actually needs — but only by having your effort move MA times farther than the load does. Multiply force by distance on both sides and the two products (the input and output work) always come out equal, in the ideal case. This is the "no free lunch" of mechanics: every simple machine in this section is really just a different way of packaging that same trade.

For math enthusiasts: where the ideal-machine equation comes from

Starting from energy conservation with no losses: Findin = Foutdout. Rearranged, Fout/Fin = din/dout — mechanical advantage (the force ratio) and velocity ratio (the distance/speed ratio) are the exact same number, just measured two different ways. That's why, in every widget in this section, computing one automatically tells you the other.

Widget 2 — Ideal vs. Real Machines

Real machines lose some input work to friction and heat. To deliver the same useful output work, a less efficient machine demands more input than the ideal case — mechanical advantage doesn't disappear, but you stop getting it for free.

For math enthusiasts: efficiency and mechanical advantage are independent

Mechanical advantage compares force in to force out; efficiency compares work out to work in. A machine can have a huge mechanical advantage and still be quite inefficient (a rusty pulley system), or a small mechanical advantage and be nearly perfectly efficient (a smooth, direct lever). The two numbers describe different things and neither one determines the other.

Levers

Make a prediction first: a crowbar, a wheelbarrow, and your own forearm are all levers, but they don't feel similar to use at all — a wheelbarrow multiplies your lifting force, while your forearm curling a dumbbell clearly does not. Are these really the same kind of machine?

τ = F·r  (torque = force × lever arm)  ·  Balance: τeffort = τload

τ — torque, the turning effect of a force about a pivot
r — the lever arm: perpendicular distance from the pivot (fulcrum) to where the force acts
Fulcrum — the pivot point the lever rotates around

All three levers in the prediction above obey the exact same balance rule; what differs is only where the fulcrum sits relative to the effort and the load. That single arrangement choice is what decides whether a lever multiplies force (like the wheelbarrow) or sacrifices force for speed and reach (like your forearm).

For math enthusiasts: why the forearm's "disadvantage" is the whole point

A third-class lever always has reffort < rload, so mechanical advantage reffort/rload is always less than 1 — it takes more force than the load itself to operate. What you get in exchange is exactly what the "no free lunch" rule promises: the load end moves through a much larger distance (and speed) than the effort end. Your forearm muscle contracts only a little, but your hand sweeps through a wide, fast arc — precisely why arms and legs are built as third-class levers, trading force for speed and reach.

Pulleys

Make a prediction first: a pulley bolted to the ceiling lets you pull a rope down to lift a load up. Does that single pulley make the load any easier to lift, or does it only change which direction you pull?

MA = number of rope segments directly supporting the load  ·  Effort force = load / MA  ·  Rope pulled = MA × load's rise

Fixed pulley — anchored in place; changes the direction of the pull, but MA = 1
Movable pulley — attached to the load itself and rises with it; each rope segment touching it shares the load's weight

Widget 1 — Fixed vs. Movable Pulley

The prediction above resolves right here: a single fixed pulley changes direction only. Attach that pulley to the load instead — a movable pulley — and two rope segments now share the load, cutting the needed force in half (at the cost of pulling twice the rope).

For math enthusiasts: rope-length constraints and the Atwood machine

Every rope segment supporting the movable block shortens by the same amount when you pull — that's the "rope-length constraint," and it's the real reason pulling in n times the rope only raises the load 1/n as far: the total rope is fixed, so it has to be divided among all n supporting segments equally. The two-hanging-masses version of a pulley system (no mechanical advantage, just redirected tension) was already built as the Atwood Machine in Newton's Laws → Tension and Connected Objects.

Try it for real: shoelace pulley

  1. Loop a shoelace or string around two pens (acting as pulleys) multiple times before attaching a small weight, and feel how much less force it takes to lift compared to pulling it directly — at the cost of pulling far more string.

Inclined Planes, Wedges, Screws, and Wheel-and-Axle

Make a prediction first: a long, gently sloped ramp and a short, steep one both get a heavy crate up to the same loading-dock height. Which ramp needs less pushing force — and does either one save you any actual work?

A ramp is a simple machine with MA = 1/sinθ (frictionless case) — the same "no free lunch" trade from Mechanical Advantage, with the ramp's length standing in for the effort distance. This tab covers ramps and wedges; screws and the wheel-and-axle — both built from the exact same wrapped or spinning inclined plane — get their own dedicated tab next.

Widget 1 — Ramp Force Calculator

Compare lifting a crate straight up against pushing it up a ramp to the same height. The friction-by-friction force breakdown for a single incline was already covered in Newton's Laws → Inclined Planes; here, the focus is purely the mechanical-advantage trade between the two methods.

Dock height, h1.2 m
Ramp angle, θ20°
Force lifting straight up400 N
Force pushing up the ramp (frictionless), Wsinθ137 N
Ramp length needed, h/sinθ3.51 m

For math enthusiasts: the ramp saves force, never work

Direct lift work: Wh. Ramp work (frictionless): (Wsinθ)×(h/sinθ) = Wh — identical. The gentler ramp needs less force but a proportionally longer push, exactly canceling out. Any force savings you actually measure on a real ramp beyond that trade is really friction working in your favor in an unusual way, or more often, friction working against you and costing extra — never a genuine reduction in total work.

Widget 2 — Wedge: Splitting Force

A wedge is just two inclined planes back to back. Driving it straight down converts a modest downward force into a much larger sideways splitting force — the thinner the wedge angle, the bigger that multiplication.

Driving force (downward)80 N
Wedge half-angle, φ10°
Splitting force (each side), F/tanφ454 N
Mechanical advantage, 1/tanφ5.67
For math enthusiasts: why axes are ground thin

As the half-angle φ shrinks toward zero, tanφ shrinks even faster (proportionally to φ itself, in radians, for small angles), so 1/tanφ grows without bound. That's why a thin axe blade splits wood so effectively compared to a blunt wedge — and also why an extremely thin blade requires an impractically long, precise taper to manufacture, a real engineering trade-off this idealized formula doesn't capture.

Screws and Wheel-and-Axle Systems

Make a prediction first: a car jack lets one person lift an entire car by turning a small handle over and over. Is the jack's screw doing something fundamentally different from the ramp in the last tab, or is it secretly the exact same trick in disguise?

A screw is an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder; a wheel-and-axle is really a lever that spins in a full circle instead of swinging back and forth. Both still reduce to force × distance staying constant.

p — thread pitch: how far a screw advances in one full turn
r — radius at which you apply the turning force (wrench, handle, wheel rim)
R — wheel radius; raxle — axle radius

Widget 1 — Screw Mechanics Visualizer

Turn the handle once and it sweeps out a full circle of circumference 2πr — while the screw itself only advances one pitch-length, p. "Unwrapped," that's exactly a ramp of length 2πr rising a height p, which is where the mechanical advantage formula below comes from directly.

Handle/wrench radius, r0.15 m
Thread pitch, p0.005 m
Axial load to overcome, W2000 N
Mechanical advantage, 2πr/p188.5
Required turning force, Wp/(2πr)10.6 N
"Unwrapped" ramp angle, atan(p/2πr)0.30°
For math enthusiasts: why a finer thread multiplies force more

Halving the pitch p without changing r doubles the mechanical advantage 2πr/p — the "ramp" the thread unwraps into gets twice as shallow for the same circumference. That's exactly why fine-threaded screws and bolts (many turns per inch) can clamp with enormous force from modest hand torque, at the cost of needing far more turns to travel any given distance.

A steering wheel, doorknob, and winch handle are all the same machine: a large wheel turning a much smaller axle multiplies the torque you apply into a much larger force (or, run the other way, turns a small effort at the rim into fast rotation at the axle).

Gears

Make a prediction first: you insert a third gear (an "idler") between two meshing gears, without changing either original gear's tooth count. Does the overall speed ratio between the first and last gear change?

ω₁N₁ = ω₂N₂ for any two meshing gears  ·  Every mesh reverses rotation direction

N — number of teeth on a gear
ω — angular speed
Idler gear — a gear inserted between two others purely to change direction or bridge a gap

Gear teeth must mesh at the same linear (tangential) speed where they touch, which forces ω₁N₁ = ω₂N₂ — a smaller gear spins proportionally faster than a larger one it's meshed with. Torque works the opposite way: the smaller, faster gear delivers proportionally less torque, same trade-off as every other simple machine in this section.

Widget 1 — Gear Train and the Idler Puzzle

For math enthusiasts: why the idler drops out of the ratio

With the idler: ωB = ωANA/NB, then ωC = ωBNB/NC = (ωANA/NB)(NB/NC) = ωANA/NC — NB cancels algebraically no matter what value it has. But each mesh (A–B, then B–C) reverses direction once, for two reversals total — back to A's original direction. Remove the idler and mesh A directly with C: exactly one reversal, so C spins opposite to A, even though its speed is identical either way.

Widget 2 — Bicycle Gears

A bicycle's chainring-and-cog pair is a gear train connected by a chain instead of meshing teeth directly, but the same ratio math applies — and it's the whole reason low gears help on hills while high gears help on flats.

For math enthusiasts: why low gears feel "easier" but not free

A small chainring-to-cog ratio multiplies your pedaling torque at the wheel (helpful for hills), but proportionally reduces wheel speed for the same cadence — you pedal the same effort, spin your legs the same speed, but travel slower per pedal stroke. As always, nothing here creates or destroys energy; it only reshapes the force/speed trade at the wheel.
