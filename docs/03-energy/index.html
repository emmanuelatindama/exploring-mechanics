3. Work, Energy, and Power

Energy methods often solve a problem more directly than force-based methods.

Work and the Work-Energy Theorem

Make a prediction first: you carry a heavy bag at a perfectly constant height across a flat room. Your arm is clearly exerting a force the whole time. Does physics count that as "doing work" on the bag?

W = F d cosθ   ·   Net work = ΔKE (the Work-Energy Theorem)

W — work done by a force (Joules, J)
F — magnitude of the applied force; d — magnitude of the displacement
θ — the angle between the force and the displacement directions
KE — kinetic energy; ΔKE — change in kinetic energy

Work only counts the part of a force that acts along the direction of motion. Carrying that bag at constant height, your upward supporting force is perpendicular to your (horizontal) walking displacement — cosθ = cos90° = 0, so despite feeling tired, you do exactly zero work on the bag. Lift it straight up instead and force and displacement point the same way — positive work. Lower it slowly against your own resistance and they point opposite ways — negative work.

For math enthusiasts: work is a dot product

W = F d cosθ is exactly the dot product F⃗·d⃗ from the Toolkit's vectors tab, applied to force and displacement specifically. That's also why work is a scalar (a single signed number, no direction of its own) even though both of its ingredients are vectors — the dot product strips away everything except "how much of one vector points along the other."

For math enthusiasts: why a spring's work formula has that extra factor of ½

A triangle's area is ½ × base × height. Here the base is the stretch x and the height is the force at that final point, kx — so work stretching a spring from 0 to x is W = ½x(kx) = ½kx². The factor of ½ isn't arbitrary; it's simply what "area of a triangle" requires once the force itself is changing linearly instead of staying constant.

Kinetic Energy

Make a prediction first: a car crashes at 60 km/h instead of 30 km/h — twice the speed. Is the energy that has to go somewhere in that crash twice as much, or something else entirely?

KE = ½mv²

KE — kinetic energy, the energy an object has because it's moving (Joules, J)
m — mass; v — speed

Kinetic energy grows with the square of speed, not speed itself. Double the speed and you don't double the kinetic energy — you quadruple it. That's the real answer to the prediction above: a crash at 60 km/h carries four times the energy of one at 30 km/h, which is exactly why speed limits matter so much more than they'd seem to at first glance.

For math enthusiasts: deriving ½mv² from the work-energy theorem

Start an object from rest and apply a constant force F over a distance d. The work-energy theorem says that work becomes its kinetic energy: KE = Fd = (ma)d. From kinematics, v² = 2ad, so ad = v²/2. Substituting: KE = m(v²/2) = ½mv². The formula isn't a separate law — it falls straight out of F = ma and the definition of work.

"Elastic" is defined as a collision where kinetic energy is conserved — it's not a coincidence, it's the definition. Real collisions (car crashes, a dropped ball of clay) are almost always somewhere between perfectly elastic and perfectly inelastic; the "lost" energy was never destroyed, it converted into heat, sound, and permanent deformation of the colliding objects.

Potential Energy and Conservation of Energy

Make a prediction first: a ball rolls back and forth in a smooth, frictionless bowl. Does it ever rise higher than the height it was released from? Does it ever stop?

Total mechanical energy = KE + PE = constant, as long as no dissipative force (like friction or air resistance) is removing energy from the system.

KE — kinetic energy, ½mv²
PEgrav — gravitational potential energy, mgh, where h is height above a chosen reference level
PEelastic — elastic potential energy stored in a stretched or compressed spring, ½kx²
Conservative forces (gravity, ideal springs) store and return energy exactly; dissipative forces (friction, drag) permanently convert it to heat

The ball in the prediction above trades kinetic energy for gravitational potential energy and back, forever, in an idealized frictionless bowl — it always returns to exactly its starting height, never higher (that would create energy from nothing) and never lower forever (nothing is removing energy). Add real friction and the bowl slowly "wins," converting mechanical energy into heat until the ball settles at the bottom.

Conservation of Energy

Make a prediction first: a braking car's speed drops from 25 m/s to 0. Its kinetic energy clearly disappears — so where, specifically, did it go?

In an isolated system (one with no energy crossing its boundary), total energy is exactly conserved — it only ever changes form. "Lost" energy is never actually destroyed; it's converted into a form that's easy to overlook, usually heat, sound, or permanent deformation.

The braking car's kinetic energy becomes heat in the brake pads and tires (and a little sound) — track your hand along a car's wheel well right after a hard stop and you can feel it directly. Widget 1 tracks exactly this kind of accounting, second by second, for two different scenarios.

For math enthusiasts: why "heat" here is just bookkeeping, not a separate force

The heat curve isn't computed from some new law — it's just whatever's missing: Heat(t) = E₀ − KE(t) − PE(t). That's the entire content of "energy is conserved": once you know how kinetic and potential energy changed, the heat term is forced, not independently measured. It's the same accounting trick used to find rope tension in the last section — solve for everything else, and the remaining unknown is whatever balances the books.

Power and Efficiency

Make a prediction first: two people each climb the same flight of stairs, lifting the same body weight the same height. One sprints up in 5 seconds; the other strolls up in 30 seconds. Did they do the same amount of work? Did they produce the same power?

P = W/t = Fv   ·   Efficiency = Pout/Pin

P — power, the rate of doing work (Watts, W = J/s)
W — work; t — time; F — force; v — speed
Efficiency — the fraction of input power that comes out as useful output power (the rest becomes waste heat)

Both stair-climbers in the prediction above do exactly the same work (same weight, same height gained) — work doesn't care about time at all. But the sprinter produces six times the power, because power is work divided by how long it took. This is the entire reason a small motor can still lift a heavy load (given enough time) while a large motor is needed to lift it quickly.

For math enthusiasts: two equivalent formulas for power

P = W/t works for a single event with a clear start and end. For continuous motion — a car cruising, a cyclist pedaling — it's often more useful to write P = Fv: since W = Fd and v = d/t, dividing both sides by t gives W/t = F(d/t) = Fv. Same physics, whichever form fits the situation better.

For math enthusiasts: why drag power scales as v³

Air-drag force already grows with v² (more air hit per second and each hit is harder). Power is force times speed, so drag power is v² × v = v³. That cubic growth is why elite cyclists spend enormous effort shaving off small amounts of drag (aerodynamic helmets, tucked positions) — at high speed, drag dominates the whole power budget.

Roller Coaster Physics

Make a prediction first: a coaster car crests the top of a hill. Do you feel heavier, lighter, or the same as standing still — and does that feeling have anything to do with your actual weight changing?

A coaster (ignoring track friction and air resistance) is a pure energy-conservation machine: ½mv² + mgh = constant along the whole ride. But what you feel isn't your speed or height — it's the track's normal force on you, your apparent weight, which can differ sharply from mg.

h — height above a reference level; v — speed
N — normal force from the seat/track on the rider; apparent weight
r — radius of curvature of the track at a hill's crest or a dip's bottom
G — apparent weight expressed as a multiple of ordinary weight, G = N/mg

For math enthusiasts: why the second hill must be shorter than the first

At the very top of the first hill the car is (approximately) at rest, so its total energy is entirely mgh1. At any later point, ½mv² + mgh = mgh1, so v² = 2g(h1 − h). For v² to stay non-negative, h can never exceed h1 — which is exactly why no later hill on a real coaster (without an extra motor) is ever built taller than the first one.

Widget 2 — Apparent Weight and G-Forces

This is the part of coaster design with real safety stakes: taking a dip or crest too fast, at too tight a radius, can push apparent weight to levels that cause tunnel vision, blackout, or (in documented historical cases of poorly-designed rides) fatal injury. Real coasters are engineered to keep momentary G-forces within a well-studied safe envelope.

For math enthusiasts: deriving apparent weight at a crest and a dip

At a hilltop, both gravity and the track's normal force point toward the center of the curve (downward), so Newton's Second Law along that direction reads mg − N = mv²/r, giving N = mg − mv²/r — less than your ordinary weight, and it can reach exactly zero (true weightlessness, momentarily) or even go negative, meaning the track would need to actively hold you down (this is exactly a vertical-circle problem, developed fully in Circular Motion → Vertical Circles). At a dip, the normal force points up while gravity points down, both relative to a center of curvature that's now above the car, giving N − mg = mv²/r, so N = mg + mv²/r — always more than ordinary weight.

Why this matters: sustained vertical G-forces above roughly 4–5G can cause a person to gray out or black out as blood is pulled away from the brain; brief peaks somewhat higher are tolerable, but design margins matter enormously — this is a large part of why coaster engineering is a licensed, heavily regulated field, and why historical rides that got this wrong have caused real injuries and deaths. Nothing here is a substitute for that engineering; it's just the same N = mg ± mv²/r relationship you can check for yourself above.

For math enthusiasts: where the 2.5r comes from

At minimum speed, gravity alone supplies the centripetal force at the loop's top: mg = mvtop²/r, so vtop² = gr. Energy conservation from the first hill (height h, at rest) to the loop's top (height 2r, speed vtop) gives mgh = mg(2r) + ½mvtop² = 2mgr + ½mgr, so h = 2.5r — the hill must be built two and a half times taller than the loop itself, regardless of the car's mass.
