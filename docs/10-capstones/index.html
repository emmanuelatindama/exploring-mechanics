Bowling Physics

Make a prediction first: a bowling ball is thrown down the lane without any spin at all, sliding at first before it starts to roll normally. Does it slide down the whole lane, or does something change partway?

v₀ — the ball's initial thrown speed with no spin; μ — kinetic friction between ball and lane; R — ball radius
Friction acts backward on the ball's motion but forward as a torque, simultaneously slowing its slide and spinning it up, until the surface speed at the contact point reaches zero — the rolling condition v = ωR

For math enthusiasts: where 5v₀/7 comes from

With v(t) = v₀ − μgt and ω(t) = (5μg/2R)t (starting from zero spin), rolling begins when v = ωR: v₀ − μgt = (5μg/2)t, giving t = v₀/(3.5μg). Substituting back, vroll = v₀ − μg·v₀/(3.5μg) = v₀(1 − 1/3.5) = 5v₀/7 — independent of μ and R entirely, though the time and distance to reach it are not.

For math enthusiasts: why the prediction above is false

A longer arm increases moment of inertia I = mL² quadratically, while it only increases the lever-arm advantage (and the tip's speed for a given ω) linearly. Since angular acceleration α = τ/I ∝ 1/L², and tip speed v = ωL with ω ∝ √α ∝ 1/L, the net launch speed actually scales as v ∝ 1/√L for a fixed torque — a longer arm gives a slower launch unless the driving torque also increases to compensate.
