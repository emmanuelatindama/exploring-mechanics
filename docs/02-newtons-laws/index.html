Exploring Mechanics / 2. Newton's Laws

2. Newton's Laws and Forces

This section explains why motion changes.

Newton's First Law: Inertia

Make a prediction first: a magician yanks a tablecloth out from under a full table setting, and the plates barely move. A driver slams the brakes, and an unbelted passenger keeps sliding forward even though the car has already stopped. What do these two tricks have in common? Both are Newton's First Law doing exactly what it says: an object keeps doing whatever it was already doing — staying still, or moving at constant velocity — until a net force makes it do otherwise.

ΣF = 0  ⟹  a = 0  ⟹  constant velocity (including velocity = 0, i.e. staying at rest)

ΣF — net (total, vector-summed) force acting on an object
a — acceleration
m — mass, the object's resistance to being accelerated ("inertia")
μ — coefficient of friction (how strongly a surface resists sliding)
g — gravitational acceleration ≈ 9.8 m/s²
v₀ — an object's velocity before any new net force acts on it

Inertia isn't a force — it's a property. It's the reason a net force is required to change motion at all, and the reason two objects of different mass respond differently to the same force. Every widget below is really the same law, staged three different ways: something continues moving (or sitting still) simply because nothing has managed to force it to do otherwise yet.

For math enthusiasts: why "no net force" includes "balanced forces," not just "no forces"

Newton's First Law is often mis-stated as "an object at rest stays at rest, and a moving object keeps moving, unless a force acts on it." The precise statement uses net force: a book sitting on a table has two real forces on it (gravity pulling down, the table pushing up) that are individually nonzero but sum to ΣF = 0 — that's why it doesn't accelerate. A hockey puck gliding at constant velocity on ice isn't force-free either (gravity and the ice's support still act on it); it's simply that those vertical forces balance and the horizontal friction force is small enough to ignore over a short glide. "Constant velocity" is the general case; "at rest" is just the special case where that constant velocity happens to be zero.

This is the tablecloth trick shrunk down to a coin balanced on a playing card over a cup's opening. Flick the card away fast enough and the coin barely moves before the card is gone — then gravity (not the flick) drops it straight into the cup. Flick too gently, or use too high a friction coefficient, and the coin gets dragged along with the card instead.

For math enthusiasts: why a faster flick makes the coin move less

While the card is still under the coin, kinetic friction is the only horizontal force on the coin, giving it acceleration a = μg — fixed, independent of how fast you flick. What changes with flick speed is time: the faster the card moves, the sooner its trailing edge clears the coin's position, so the coin only gets to accelerate for a shorter contact time tcontact. Its sideways drift during contact is ½at² — squared in a quantity that's shrinking — so a faster flick reduces drift dramatically, not just a little. That's the whole trick: outrun the friction, not overpower it.

Widget 2 — Passenger Lurch During Braking

A car brakes hard. Its unbelted passenger doesn't get pushed forward by any force — nothing is pushing them — they simply keep moving at the speed they already had, per Newton's First Law, while the car (which the brakes are acting on) slows down underneath them. From inside the car, that looks exactly like being "thrown forward." Toggle the seatbelt to see what force it takes to make the passenger decelerate along with the car instead.

For math enthusiasts: the passenger's drift is exactly the gap between two motions

Without a belt, the passenger has zero net horizontal force on them (ignoring the small friction of a car seat), so by the First Law their position is just xpax(t) = v₀t — unchanged constant-velocity motion. The car, acted on by the brakes, follows xcar(t) = v₀t − ½at². The passenger's drift relative to the car's cabin is the difference: Δx = xpax − xcar = ½at², growing the whole time the car is still decelerating. With the seatbelt on, the belt supplies exactly the extra backward force needed to give the passenger the same deceleration a as the car, so Δx stays at essentially zero — the belt is what turns "passenger" and "car" into one connected system.

Try it for real: card-and-coin and the tablecloth pull

  1. Balance a coin on an index card over the mouth of a cup or glass. Flick the card horizontally, fast and flat, with one finger — the harder and flatter the flick, the more reliably the coin drops straight in.
  2. The full-size version is the classic tablecloth trick: a smooth tablecloth, light dishware, and a fast, flat, level pull. Practice with plastic cups before risking anything breakable.
  3. Both rely on the same limit: the shorter the time friction has to act on the object left behind, the smaller its sideways drift — exactly the ½at² relationship from Widget 1.

Newton's Second Law

Make a prediction first: you push an empty shopping cart and a fully loaded one with the exact same effort. Which one speeds up faster? Now push the empty cart twice as hard. Does it speed up twice as fast, or by some other factor? Newton's Second Law answers both questions with one equation.

F = ma

F — net force acting on the object (Newtons, N)
m — mass (kilograms, kg) — the object's resistance to acceleration
a — acceleration (m/s²) — how quickly velocity changes
μ — coefficient of kinetic friction
g — gravitational acceleration ≈ 9.8 m/s²

Rearranged as a = F/m, the law says acceleration is directly proportional to net force and inversely proportional to mass. Double the force on the same object and it accelerates twice as fast. Double the mass under the same force and it accelerates half as fast. Neither relationship is obvious until you've felt both — which is exactly what Widget 1 lets you compare directly.

For math enthusiasts: where "net" force comes from and why mass, not weight, sits in the equation

F here is always the net (vector-summed) force — if three forces pull an object in different directions, you add them as vectors first, then apply F = ma to that single resultant. Also note the equation uses m (mass, an intrinsic property, the same on the Moon as on Earth), not weight (mg, which depends on local gravity). A given net force produces the exact same acceleration on the Moon as on Earth, even though that object weighs six times less there — mass is what actually resists acceleration, not weight.

Try it for real: loaded vs. empty cart

  1. Find a shopping cart, wagon, or wheeled office chair. Give it a push with roughly the same effort, empty and then loaded with something heavy, and compare how quickly each picks up speed.

Newton's Third Law

Make a prediction first: two ice skaters of very different sizes push off from each other, each using exactly the same push. Does the lighter skater feel a bigger shove than the heavier one? Or do they experience the same force, and just respond to it differently?

For every force one object exerts on a second object, the second exerts a force equal in magnitude and opposite in direction back on the first.

FA on B — force object A exerts on object B
FB on A — force object B exerts on object A; always FB on A = −FA on B
m — mass; v — velocity gained; Δt — contact/push duration

Common misconception: action-reaction pairs do not cancel out, because they act on two different objects. A force only cancels another force acting on the same object. The push you feel and the push you give are a real pair of equal-and-opposite forces that never cancel — they just act on you and on whatever you pushed, separately.

For math enthusiasts: equal forces, unequal accelerations

The forces are always equal (FA on B = −FB on A), but Newton's Second Law (a = F/m) still applies separately to each object with its own mass. Equal force, different mass, therefore different acceleration — the lighter skater always flies off faster than the heavier one, even though the push between them was perfectly equal in both directions the entire time.

Swap "skater B" for "the entire Earth" (via the wall you're pushing, bolted to the ground) and the force pair is still exactly equal: you push the wall with some force, the wall pushes back on you with the same force, which is why you feel resistance at all. But Earth's mass is on the order of 10²⁴ kg, so its resulting acceleration a = F/m is immeasurably tiny — not zero, just far too small to ever notice. The law doesn't stop applying; the effect just becomes invisible at extreme mass ratios.

  1. Thread a straw onto a long piece of string pulled taut between two chairs. Tape an inflated (but unknotted) balloon to the straw, then let go — the air rushing out one way pushes the balloon the other way.

Friction

Make a prediction first: you push a heavy box that doesn't budge. You push a little harder — still nothing. You push just a little harder still, and suddenly it lurches forward far more easily than you expected. Why does it take a bigger push to start the box moving than it does to keep it moving?

Static friction: fs ≤ μsN  (matches the applied push, up to a maximum)  ·  Kinetic friction: fk = μkN  (roughly constant while sliding)

fs, fk — static and kinetic friction force
μs, μk — coefficients of static and kinetic friction (usually μk ≤ μs)
N — normal force (here, on flat ground, N = mg)
m — mass; g — gravitational acceleration ≈ 9.8 m/s²

Friction isn't a fixed drag that always opposes you by some constant amount — static friction is more like a rubber band that stretches to match whatever push you apply, up to a maximum of μsN. Push harder than that maximum and the object breaks free; friction switches to kinetic friction, which is usually a bit smaller. That drop is exactly why a stuck box suddenly "lurches" the instant it starts moving.

For math enthusiasts: why friction depends on normal force, not on contact area or speed

Both friction laws are empirical (not derived from something deeper) but they fit real surfaces surprisingly well: friction force is proportional to how hard the two surfaces are pressed together (N), essentially independent of the visible contact area and, for kinetic friction, roughly independent of sliding speed too. A heavier box needs a bigger push to start sliding, not because it has more surface area, but purely because its weight increases N.

For math enthusiasts: friction as both a helpful and a resistive force

Friction resists your push on this box — but it's also the only reason walking, climbing a slope, or a car's tires gripping the road work at all. Your foot pushes backward on the ground; without friction, the ground couldn't push forward on you in return (that's Newton's Third Law again), and you'd simply slip in place. "Friction is bad" and "friction is necessary" are both true depending entirely on whether you're trying to prevent sliding or trying to cause it.

Widget 3 — Traction Limit: Braking and Turning

Braking distance and how sharply you can turn are governed by exactly the same number: how much friction the tires can supply. (Reaction time and raw stopping distance for a chosen deceleration were covered back in Kinematics → Constant Acceleration; here, the deceleration itself comes directly from road friction.)

For math enthusiasts: the "traction circle" idea

A tire can only supply so much total friction force before it slides, no matter which direction that force points — braking, turning, or (realistically) some mix of both at once. That's why racing drivers brake in a straight line before a turn rather than mid-corner: braking friction and cornering friction draw from the same limited budget, roughly μmg total, in any direction. Try it for real: starting friction vs. sliding friction

  1. Push a heavy book across a table, starting from rest each time. Notice the little "catch" right before it starts moving — that's static friction at its maximum, just before it lets go.
  2. Once it's sliding, try to keep pushing with steady, gentle force — it usually takes noticeably less effort to keep it going than it took to start it.

Inclined Planes

Make a prediction first: a box sits on a ramp. As you slowly tilt the ramp steeper, at some angle it suddenly starts sliding. Does that critical angle depend on how heavy the box is?

On a slope, gravity splits into two components: mg sinθ (pulling the box down the slope) and mg cosθ (pressing the box into the slope, which is exactly what the normal force N has to balance).

θ — the ramp's angle above horizontal
N — normal force, perpendicular to the slope; here N = mg cosθ
μs, μk — static and kinetic friction coefficients
m — mass; g — gravitational acceleration ≈ 9.8 m/s²
For math enthusiasts: decomposing gravity along tilted axes

Gravity itself never changes — it's always mg straight down. What changes is which directions are convenient to measure it in. Tilting your axes to match the slope (one axis along the surface, one perpendicular to it) turns a single vector into two components, mg sinθ and mg cosθ, using the same right-triangle trigonometry as the vector components in the Toolkit — just with gravity playing the role of the vector being split, and the slope's tilt playing the role of the angle.

For math enthusiasts: deriving θ꜀ = atan(μₛ)

Sliding begins exactly when mgsinθ = μₛN = μₛmgcosθ. The mass m and g cancel from both sides — which is the answer to the prediction question: the critical angle never depends on the box's weight. What's left is sinθ = μₛcosθ, or tanθ = μₛ, so θ꜀ = atan(μₛ). A rougher surface (larger μₛ) tolerates a steeper ramp before sliding starts.

Try it for real: find your own critical angle

  1. Rest a coin or small object on a book. Slowly lift one end until the object just starts to slide, and note the angle (a phone's level/angle app works well for this).

Tension and Connected Objects

Make a prediction first: a heavy block and a light block hang from opposite ends of a rope over a pulley. Does the heavier one accelerate downward as fast as it would falling freely, slower, or not at all?

A rope is treated as massless (its own weight is negligible) and inextensible (it doesn't stretch), which forces two objects tied together to share exactly the same speed and the same magnitude of acceleration at every instant — they move as a single connected system, even though the rope pulls on each one from a different direction.

T — tension: the pulling force a rope transmits, the same magnitude throughout an ideal massless rope
a — the common acceleration shared by every object tied into the system
m₁, m₂ — the masses of the two connected objects

The general strategy: draw a separate free-body diagram for each object, write F = ma for each one with the same unknown a and the same unknown T appearing in both equations (pulling in opposite, connected directions), then solve the two equations together.

For math enthusiasts: why tension isn't just "half the weight" or some other shortcut

A common guess is that the tension in a hanging system just equals one of the weights, or their average. It doesn't, in general — tension is whatever value makes both connected objects share the exact same acceleration, and that value comes out of solving the system, not from either mass alone. Widget 2 below lets you check this directly: set the two hanging masses equal and the system sits in perfect balance with T = mg exactly; make them unequal and T lands somewhere between the two weights, but never exactly at their average.

One more thing a rope can't do: push. It only ever pulls, along its own length. (If you swapped the rope in Widget 1 for a spring instead, pulling it and letting go wouldn't give one clean acceleration — it would set up a back-and-forth oscillation, the subject of its own full section later: Oscillations → Simple Harmonic Motion.)

Widget 2 — Atwood Machine (Two Hanging Masses)

Two masses hang from a rope over a pulley. If they're equal, nothing moves. Make one heavier and the whole system accelerates — but (answering the prediction above) always slower than free fall, because the rope is also busy lifting the lighter mass.

For math enthusiasts: solving the Atwood machine

Taking "A descending" as positive: for A, mAg − T = mAa; for B (moving up), T − mBg = mBa. Adding the two equations cancels T entirely, giving a = (mA−mB)g / (mA+mB). Substituting back gives T = 2mAmBg / (mA+mB) — notice this is always less than either individual weight, which is exactly why the heavier side never falls as fast as it would in free fall: the rope is holding it back precisely as much as it's lifting the other side.
